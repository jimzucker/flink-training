# Block-trade allocation and running positions: does it scale?

**1→2 cores: 2.01× (101% of linear), range 1.94–2.08× across passes.**
**2→4 cores: 2.15× (108% of linear), range 2.04–2.27× across passes.**

Buying a second core and a second degree of parallelism bought a second core's
worth of block trades; buying the third and fourth bought at least their own
worth again. Every case ran with the worker at 99.4–100.3% of its CPU cap and
throttled in ~100% of scheduler periods, so the worker — not the broker, not the
job manager, not the disk — was the thing constrained. **The 2→4 step came out
above linear and I cannot explain it; see "What I did not explain".**

Scope: this is one pipeline on one laptop. It supports *"this pipeline scaled
linearly from one to four cores on one worker"*, not *"Flink scales"*.

## The pipeline

One block trade in — a 22-field FIX-derived JSON message, ~430 B — and four
records out:

```
V1  Source: kafka-source -> parse-block-trade -> allocate      (chained)
V2  [HASH by symbol]   symbol-position  -> sink positions-by-symbol
V3  [HASH by account]  account-position -> sink positions-by-account
```

Each block trade is allocated pro rata across 3 of 4096 accounts; the slices sum
to the block quantity exactly. The symbol path aggregates the **block trade**,
the account path aggregates the **allocations**, so the two are independent
paths over the same input and must agree — which is what completeness checks.
Both emit the absolute running position (net quantity, net notional in integer
cents, count) keyed on the Kafka record, so a replayed write is the same number
again rather than a double count. The graph is three vertices at every
parallelism; the harness read the shape off the running plan on every case and
would have refused a row whose shape differed.

Cardinality: 512 symbols, 4096 accounts — predicted before the run and asserted
against the sinks.

## What was refused, and why

| what | when | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| **1 core, p3-asc** — worker used 95.9% of its 1-core cap, floor 98% | in the suite | A *data* refusal about one case, so the harness marked that pass and carried on. The 1-core case still had 2 valid passes, which is the minimum, so it stayed reportable at 2.1% spread. |
| **1 core, sentinel** — 97.4% of cap, floor 98% | last measurement of the suite | Same refusal. It cost the suite its drift check: the sentinel exists so that the first and last measurements are the same case, and with it refused, drift across the suite shows only as spread. |
| the whole tuning phase | before the chain | Four hypotheses about why the worker would not saturate were tested and **wrong**: host/VM CPU contention (VM was 4.74 of 8 cores busy), unaligned checkpoints (95.0% vs 95.4%), checkpoint interval 10 s vs 20 s (92.5% vs 91.4%), and larger sink batches (378k rec/s vs 477k, GC 9.1%). They are in `JOURNAL.md` so the two things that *were* true are not mistaken for the first guesses. |

Both refusals are the same guard on the same case. The 1-core case is the
structurally weakest one — three task threads and the Kafka client I/O threads
share a single core, and the ~2–4% it cannot fill is the moments when all of
them are blocked at once. The skill says not to run a one-unit case when the
claim starts at two units; it was run because the claim asked for 1→2, and it
is the only case that ever missed a floor.

Nothing was refused about the **rig**, so the suite never stopped early.

## What the numbers are gated on

- **Completeness passed for this build, with no tolerances**, before any
  throughput was published: 8,000,000 block trades drained to the last record
  twice — once clean, once with the task manager `docker kill`ed at 3,433,811
  records committed and the job recovering on a replacement worker. Both arms:
  512 symbol keys and 4096 account keys exactly as predicted; every per-key net
  quantity, net notional and count equal to the generator's manifest; 24,000,000
  allocations; and the two paths agreeing exactly on net quantity
  (31,875,300 shares) and net notional (1,023,225,805,300 cents).
- **The tiny proof passed**: two cases on a 70M backlog, 1→4 = 3.506× inside the
  3.00–5.00 bound, both at ~99.8% of cap, and a disk projection from the
  measured record shape (134 B/record in, 104 B of sink per input → 50.9 GB
  needed against 136.7 GB free) taken *before* the fill.
- **28 of 28 guards fired on purpose** in the live self-test, including a wrong
  CPU cap, a busy cluster, a truncated backlog, a dead sampler, a job that never
  reached RUNNING, and a host-side watcher outliving the run.
- **Two vantage points agreed** on every reported case: committed source offsets
  against sink growth divided by the fan-out, worst disagreement 0.51%.
- **Every case had backlog headroom at window close** — 222 s at the tightest,
  against a 10 s checkpoint interval.

## Where it stops

Not at the broker. Holding the worker at 4 cores and starving the broker in
steps:

| broker cap | broker used | worker | records/s |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3.0 | 0.74 (25%) | 100.2% of 4 | 335,966 |
| 1.0 | 0.71 (72%) | 100.1% of 4 | 316,700 |
| 0.5 | 0.50 (100%) | 100.0% of 4 | 298,137 |

Even pinned at 100% of half a core, the broker never took the constraint off the
worker — the worker held its full cap and gave up 11% of its rate. One broker
core comfortably serves this pipeline at four worker cores, so on this rig the
next thing to run out is the laptop: 8 vCPUs, of which the 4-core case already
uses 4 for the worker, 0.75 for the broker and 0.5 for the job manager. An
8-core case is not measurable here, so **4 cores is where this study stops, not
where the pipeline stops.**

## What I did not explain

**The 2→4 step is above linear (2.15×, 108%) and I do not know why.** A
superlinear ratio is a defect report, and every prior run that produced one
found an artefact. Here is what the data rules out and what it does not.

Ruled out, with evidence in the table:

- *The cap did not apply.* Read back from `NanoCpus` and cgroup `cpu.max` on
  every case; measured consumption 2.00/2 and 4.01/4.
- *The graph differed between cases.* Vertex count and edge ship strategies read
  off the running plan and compared; a difference is a rig refusal and none fired.
- *The rate came from the engine.* It came from committed broker offsets, and a
  second vantage point (sink growth ÷ 4) agreed within 0.5%.
- *The broker was the ceiling at 2 cores.* It used 0.30 of a 3-core cap there,
  and the ceiling run shows it does not become the constraint even at 0.5 cores.
- *Order effects.* Descending/ascending is 1.006 at 2 cores and 1.007 at 4.

Not ruled out, and the most likely candidate: **the rig drifted across the
suite.** The three 4-core passes rose monotonically — 311,070 → 322,823 →
330,331 (+6.2%) — while the three 2-core passes fell monotonically — 152,543 →
149,812 → 145,325 (−4.7%). Taken from **pass 1 alone**, where all three cases
were measured inside twelve minutes, 2→4 is **2.039×**, the bottom of the
reported range and barely above linear. The sentinel that exists to catch
exactly this drift was refused on the cap floor, so I have spread but no drift
reading. I have not identified what accumulated.

One observation I am *not* offering as a mechanism, because it is one arm of a
comparison I never ran: the 2-core case's source idled 5.3% of its window
against 0.1% at 1 core and 2.2% at 4. It is the one column where 2 cores is the
odd case out. Whether that is cause, effect, or noise would take a controlled
run to say, and I did not run one.
