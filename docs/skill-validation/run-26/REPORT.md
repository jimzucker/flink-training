# Block-trade allocation and position keeping — does it scale from 1 to 2 to 4 cores?

> **QUICK LOOK — not a publishable result.** This was run as
> `prove.py all --quick`, which measures **two** passes per case instead of the
> configured three. The harness stamps the table `publishable: false` and it is
> repeated here: two passes give a spread, not a result. The 4-core case's own
> spread here is 14.6%, which is most of the interval below.

## The outcome

**2→4 cores: 1.71× — 86% of linear. The claim is NOT met.**
**1→2 cores: 2.27× — 113% of linear. The claim is met.**

The claim, written before anything was built, was: *"This block-trade allocation
and position-keeping pipeline scales linearly from 1 to 2 to 4 cores on one
Flink task manager: each doubling of cores and parallelism returns at least 95%
of double the throughput."* On this rig, on this build, that is true from one
core to two and **false from two to four**.

The table is sound — every case held ~100% of its CPU cap, the broker never rose
above 0.36 of its 2.5 cores, the source never idled, the two vantage points
agreed to within 0.51%, and completeness passed with no tolerances for the same
build. A valid table that says the pipeline does not scale is a result about the
pipeline, not a table to withhold.

**Scope:** this is one pipeline on one laptop. It supports *"this pipeline
scaled 2.27× from 1 to 2 cores and 1.71× from 2 to 4 on this rig"*. It says
nothing about Flink.

## The step ratios, with their intervals

The claim is judged on the **lower bound** of each ratio's interval, not the
point estimate — a ratio that *might* be linear has not been shown to be. The
interval comes from the spread of the adjacent-in-time pairs.

| step | ratio | of linear | adjacent pairs | interval (95%) | lower bound as % of linear | meets the claim (floor 95%)? |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---:|---|
| **1→2** | **2.271×** | 113.5% | 2.210, 2.332 | **2.152 – 2.390** | 107.6% | **yes** |
| **2→4** | **1.711×** | 85.5% | 1.592, 1.829 | **1.479 – 1.943** | 73.9% | **no** — short by 26.1 points |

Range across passes: 1→2 spans 2.21–2.33×; 2→4 spans 1.58–1.84×. Note that the
2→4 interval's *upper* bound (1.943×, 97%) would clear the floor. Two passes
cannot separate those two answers, which is precisely why `--quick` is not
publishable.

## Garbage collection per case

| cores | GC, as the harness computes it | per-pass |
|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | **7.46%** of capacity | 8.17% (p1-asc), 6.74% (sentinel) |
| 2 | **4.69%** | 4.99%, 4.40% |
| 4 | **2.24%** | 1.96%, 2.52% |

Every case is under the harness's 11% ceiling, and inside the 0.7–9.6% band the
harness's record says well-behaved cases sit in. GC falls monotonically as
cores are added, so memory pressure is *worse* at the small cases and cannot be
what holds the 4-core case back.

One caveat, because it changes how the numbers read: Flink 1.20 publishes
`Status.JVM.GarbageCollector.All.Time` **alongside** `G1 Young Generation.Time`
and `G1 Old Generation.Time`, and the harness sums every metric ending in
`.Time`. Young + Old + All = twice the real GC time. The figures above are left
exactly as the harness computes them, because that is the figure its ceiling
judges — but the true wall-clock GC is about half of each: ~3.7%, ~2.3%, ~1.1%.

**Guards that fired on purpose:** the tiny proof's self-test broke **38 of 38**
guards and every one refused as designed — wrong CPU cap, busy cluster,
truncated backlog, dead sampler, no job running, host-side watcher, disk
overrun, spread ceiling, single pass, sentinel drift, split commit boundary,
noisy warm-up. A guard that has never fired is a guess.

**The correctness gate.** Completeness passed for this build on both arms — a
clean drain and a drain with the task manager killed at 35%. In the killed arm
the sink received **10,068,262** records for **10,000,000** trades (68,262
replayed duplicates) and the final position of all 64 symbols and all 8,192
(account, symbol) keys still matched the generator's manifest exactly, as did
the cross-check that the account positions of each symbol sum to that symbol's
position in both quantity and notional. No tolerances anywhere.

## What I did not explain

**Why 2→4 returns 86% instead of ~100%: I do not know.**

What is ruled out, with evidence from this table:

- **not the broker's CPU** — 0.36 of 2.5 cores at the 4-core case;
- **not a starved input** — source idle 0.0% at every case, and back-pressure
  *falls* with cores (56.9% → 46.3% → 31.6%);
- **not memory** — GC falls with cores (7.5% → 4.7% → 2.2%), so the largest
  case is the least pressured;
- **not the graph** — the job graph shape was read off the running plan and is
  identical at 1, 2 and 4 (three vertices, both keyBy edges HASH at every
  parallelism, so the baseline is not a chained special build);
- **not an uneven read** — 8 partitions divide evenly by 1, 2 and 4;
- **not measurement drift alone** — the sentinel drifted −4.6%, well inside the
  noise floor.

What is *context, not cause*: the harness's own probe ran in the same image
under the same caps on this machine and measured what its cores do at all —
register-only 2→4 = **98%**, memory-bound 2→4 = **76%**. This pipeline's 86%
sits between those two bounds. That says a memory-touching pipeline could not
have reached 95% here; it does not say this one was limited by memory
bandwidth. Establishing that needs one rig, one build, one variable changed and
both arms measured, and I did not run it.

Two other things I did not explain:

- **1→2 is superlinear (2.27×, 113%)**, above even this host's register-only
  bound. The one-core case is the structurally weakest — it pays every fixed
  cost (source thread, checkpoint thread, Kafka producer I/O threads, GC
  threads) on the single core it also does the work on, and its 56.9%
  source back-pressure and 7.5% GC are consistent with that. Consistent is not
  measured. The skill's §5 says outright not to run a one-unit case unless the
  claim needs it; it was run because the task asked for 1, 2 and 4.
- **The 4-core case's 14.6% spread**, driven entirely by order: 501,184 rec/s
  ascending against 580,362 descending, a 15.8% order effect at 4 cores and
  0.8% at 2. Something warms or accumulates between cases at the largest case
  only. Two passes cannot tell an order effect from noise, and I did not run
  the third.

## Where it stops

At four cores the worker is still the constraint (100.2% of cap) and the broker
is nowhere near its CPU cap (0.36 of 2.5) — so the CPU ceiling is above four
cores and was not reached. What *is* close is the broker's **memory**: at one
core, one pass out of two hit the 4 GiB cgroup limit 3,146 times and was
classified a ceiling. On a 7,838 MiB VM that already gives 4,096m to the worker
and 4 GiB to the broker, the next case up is a memory budget problem before it
is a CPU one. Whether raising `kafkaMemory` to the 6,400m the refusal named
would change the ratio is untested.
