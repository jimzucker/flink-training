Nine tips for benchmarking Flink SQL on Confluent Cloud

We benchmarked one Flink SQL statement on a laptop and on Confluent Cloud, measuring how its throughput grew as we added compute. The SQL needed no changes, but several Confluent Cloud settings did.

Swipe for the nine tips →

TIP 1 OF 9

Make the job use the whole compute pool

Confluent's autoscaler decides how much of a pool a job uses. In a 20 CFU pool it used 10 and still reported everything as OK. Set the statement's baseline_cfu to the pool's size, and read it back to check.

20 CFU pool, left to the autoscaler: 10 used
With baseline_cfu = 20: all 20 used

TIP 2 OF 9

Choose a partition count that divides evenly by every pool size

Each worker reads a share of the topic's partitions. With 6 partitions and 4 workers, two workers get 2 and two get 1. The job runs at the pace of its busiest workers, so 4 workers do the work of 3.

6 partitions over 4 workers. Pick a count every pool size divides into, such as 40 for pools of 5, 10 and 20.

TIP 3 OF 9

Give the Kafka cluster enough capacity for your largest pool

A Flink benchmark needs Kafka to keep up, or it measures Kafka instead. Confluent measures Kafka capacity in eCKU; if you cap it to control cost, set the cap for your largest test.

TIP 4 OF 9

Count your test data by reading it, not from the topic's size

A topic's size also includes records whose write was cancelled, and Flink skips those. A benchmark needs to know exactly how many records it starts with.

TIP 5 OF 9

Check your results with a snapshot query

After a benchmark you count records to make sure nothing was lost. A normal Flink SQL count sends a new running total for every record, and you have to read through all of them to reach the final number.

Set sql.snapshot.mode = now

TIP 6 OF 9

Create a new compute pool for each size you test

Confluent won't let you lower a pool's maximum size, so to test a smaller size after a larger one you need a new pool. We used a new pool for every size we tested.

TIP 8 OF 9

Make sure a benchmark deletes what it creates, even if it crashes

A test that stops halfway can leave a cluster running, and billing, for hours.
